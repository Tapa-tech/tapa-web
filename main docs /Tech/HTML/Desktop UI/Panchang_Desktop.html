How to Read Today's Panchang

Tithi, Paksha, Nakshatra explained — so you can read any Panchang entry and understand what it is telling you.

JUMP TO ☀️ What it is 🖐️ The 5 Limbs 📖 How to read ❓ Confusions 🔗 Related

Panch means five. Ang means limb.

A Panchang is a five-limbed almanac — a daily reading of where the Sun and Moon are, what kind of day that creates, and what it means for anyone who wants to align their actions with the rhythms the tradition recognises.

If you have seen the Panchang block on this app — the one showing today's tithi, paksha, nakshatra and sunrise — you may have wondered what those terms actually mean, why they change every day, and why they matter for deciding when to observe a vrat, perform a puja, or begin something new.

This guide explains each element plainly. No jargon without a definition. No astrology without an explanation. By the end you will be able to read any Panchang entry — on this app, on Drik Panchang, or on a printed Hindu calendar — and understand what it is telling you.

+The Five Limbs — What Each One Tracks
1
Tithi तिथि — The Lunar Day
What it measures: the angular distance between the Sun and the Moon

A tithi is one unit of lunar time. Every 12° of angular distance between the Sun and the Moon creates one tithi. There are 30 tithis in a full lunar month — 15 in the waxing half (Shukla Paksha) and 15 in the waning half (Krishna Paksha).

Unlike a Gregorian date, which starts and ends at midnight, a tithi starts and ends at sunrise. And because the Moon's speed varies, a tithi is not always exactly 24 hours — it can be shorter or longer. This is why a festival can sometimes 'start' on one Gregorian date and 'end' on another, or why two different panchangs might show different dates for the same festival depending on which city's sunrise they use.

Why it matters — almost every Hindu festival and vrat is fixed by tithi, not by Gregorian date. Ekadashi is the 11th tithi. Chaturthi is the 4th. Purnima is the 15th of Shukla Paksha. Amavasya is the 15th of Krishna Paksha. When someone says 'Ganesh Chaturthi falls on 14 September this year,' what they mean is: the Chaturthi tithi of Bhadrapada Shukla Paksha, mapped to the Gregorian calendar for Delhi-NCR, falls on 14 September.

2
Paksha पक्ष — The Fortnight
What it measures: which half of the lunar month we are in

Every lunar month has two halves — two pakshas. Shukla Paksha is the bright half: the Moon is waxing, growing from new moon to full moon. Krishna Paksha is the dark half: the Moon is waning, shrinking from full moon to new moon.

The Purnimanta calendar (used in North India, including Delhi-NCR) counts the month from one Purnima to the next — so the month begins with Krishna Paksha and ends with Shukla Paksha. The Amanta calendar (used in Maharashtra, Gujarat, and parts of South India) counts from one Amavasya to the next — so the month begins with Shukla Paksha.

Why it matters — when a Panchang says 'Bhadrapada Shukla Tritiya,' it is telling you: the 3rd tithi of the bright half of the month of Bhadrapada. The Paksha tells you which fortnight. Without it, you would not know whether a Chaturthi is Ganesh Chaturthi (Shukla) or Sankashti Chaturthi (Krishna).
3
Nakshatra नक्षत्र — The Lunar Mansion
What it measures: which star constellation the Moon is passing through today

The sky, as the tradition divides it, has 27 nakshatras — 27 segments of the zodiac, each about 13°20' wide. The Moon passes through all 27 in roughly one lunar month, spending approximately one day in each.

Each nakshatra has a name, a presiding deity, and a set of qualities the tradition associates with it. Some are considered auspicious for new beginnings — Pushya, Rohini, Ashwini. Others are considered unsuitable for certain activities, like Moola for starting something new. The Panchang entry for any day tells you which nakshatra the Moon is currently in.

Why it matters — nakshatra is used alongside tithi to determine festival dates. Janmashtami, for example, is not just Bhadrapada Krishna Ashtami; it is traditionally observed when the Moon is in Rohini Nakshatra. Muhurat selection relies heavily on the nakshatra of the day. And your own birth nakshatra — the one the Moon was in when you were born — is used across Jyotish tradition for name selection, compatibility, and life-stage ceremonies.

4
Vara वार — The Weekday
What it measures: the day of the week, with its presiding graha (planet)

The seven days of the week are named for the seven classical grahas. Each day carries the associations of its presiding graha.

Why it matters — specific vrats are tied to specific varas: Somvaar Vrat for Shiva, Mangalvaar for Hanuman, Guruvaar for Vishnu/Brihaspati, Shanivaar for Shani. The vara also matters for Pradosh Vrat — Bhauma Pradosh on a Tuesday is considered especially potent — and for determining Rahu Kaal, the daily period many people avoid for important decisions.
5
Yoga & Karana योग · करण — The Finer Grain
What they measure: luni-solar combinations and half-tithi periods

Yoga is calculated by adding the longitudes of the Sun and Moon and dividing by 13°20'. There are 27 yogas. Some — Siddha, Amrita, Shubha — are considered highly auspicious. Others — Vyatipata, Vaidhriti — are avoided for important beginnings. Most daily Panchang entries show the current yoga and when it transitions.

Karana is half a tithi. There are two karanas in every tithi, making roughly 60 in a lunar month. Of the 11 named karanas, 7 are 'movable' and repeat, while 4 are 'fixed' and occur only once per month. Karanas are used for fine-grained timing within a day.

If you are reading a Panchang for daily use — tithi, paksha, nakshatra and vara are sufficient. Yoga and karana become important when selecting a precise muhurat for a life event: a wedding, a griha pravesh, a business launch. Read the first four limbs, and return to these two when the need arises.

If the entry for a day reads —

Bhadrapada Shukla Chaturthi · Rohini Nakshatra · Sunrise 6:12 AM

— it is telling you: today is the 4th tithi of the bright half of the month of Bhadrapada, the Moon is in Rohini, and the day began at 6:12 AM.

If you recognise that Bhadrapada Shukla Chaturthi is Ganesh Chaturthi, you now know the festival falls today — and you know it not because someone told you, but because you read the Panchang yourself.

+Myths & Facts
✕"The festival date is different on two Panchang apps. One of them must be wrong."
✓Both may be correct — for their respective cities. Tithi transitions happen at specific moments in time, but the sunrise that marks the start of the Hindu day varies by location. A tithi that starts before sunrise in Delhi may start after sunrise in Mumbai. This is why Drik Panchang asks you to set your city, and why two Panchangs can show different dates for the same festival. Always check for your city.
✕"Purnimanta and Amanta — which calendar is the right one?"
✓Neither is 'right'. They are two conventions used in different regions. Purnimanta (North India, including Delhi-NCR) counts the month from Purnima to Purnima. Amanta (Maharashtra, Gujarat, parts of South India) counts from Amavasya to Amavasya. The tithis and nakshatras are identical — only the month name attached to a given fortnight may differ. The Tapa Co. uses Purnimanta for Delhi-NCR and notes where Amanta differs.
✕"Rahu Kaal — should I really avoid doing anything during it?"
✓Rahu Kaal is a daily period of roughly 1.5 hours, computed from sunrise and varying by weekday. Many people avoid starting important new activities during this window. Whether you observe it is a personal choice — the tradition recommends caution during this period, not prohibition. The Panchang shows the timing; what you do with it is yours to decide.
+What's Data and What's Tradition

The Panchang is a calendar. What people do with its data is tradition. The two are different authorities, and it is worth being able to tell them apart.

PANCHANG DATA
Factual — the calendar itself, computed
Tithi, Paksha, Nakshatra, Vara, Yoga, Karana — computed positions of the Sun and Moon.
Sunrise and sunset — the boundaries of the Hindu day.
Rahu Kaal timing — computed from sunrise, varies by weekday.
PRATHA
Folk interpretation layered on top of the data
'Rahu Kaal is dangerous — nothing good can happen during it.' The Panchang computes the timing; the fear-interpretation is folk tradition.
'Amavasya is unlucky for everything.' The Panchang marks it as the new moon; the 'unlucky' label is Pratha.
'You must check the Panchang before doing anything important.' A valid cultural practice — but the Panchang itself does not demand it.
'Certain Nakshatras are bad — avoid them completely.' The Panchang lists nakshatras; the good/bad framing varies by interpreter.
The Panchang is a tool. It describes the quality of time. What you do with that description — whether you avoid Rahu Kaal, plan around nakshatras, or simply note what tithi it is — is your choice, informed by your family's tradition. The Panchang provides the data. The tradition provides the interpretation.

The Panchang is not a fortune-telling device. It is a calendar — one that tracks the Sun and the Moon instead of just the Sun, and that encodes thousands of years of observation about how these movements relate to the rhythms of life on Earth. Reading it does not require belief in prediction. It requires only the willingness to notice that time is not uniform — that some days carry different qualities than others, and that the tradition has developed a precise vocabulary for describing those qualities.

Five limbs. Tithi tells you the lunar day. Paksha tells you the fortnight. Nakshatra tells you the Moon's position. Vara tells you the planetary ruler. Yoga and Karana refine the picture. Together they give you a complete reading of today — not what will happen, but what kind of day it is. The rest is up to you.
