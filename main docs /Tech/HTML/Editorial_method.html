OUR EDITORIAL METHOD

How we decide what is true

Every claim on this platform is placed in one of three categories, and most carry a confidence score. This page explains what those mean, where our sources come from, and how to tell us we have got something wrong.

If you arrived here by tapping "Read source" on an article — this is the right place. The specific text for that claim is named on the article itself; this page explains the system behind it.

THE PROBLEM THIS SOLVES
Most ritual advice does not tell you where it came from

Someone tells you a vrat must be kept without water. Someone else says a particular day is unlucky. A forwarded message warns you what happens if you skip a step. None of it says whether it comes from a text, from a region, from a family — or from nowhere at all.

That is the gap. Not a shortage of information about Hindu ritual, but no way to tell which kind of information you are looking at.

So we label it. Every factual claim in every guide is placed in one of three categories, visible on the page, before you decide what to do with it.

SECTION 01
The three categories

These are not degrees of truth. They are different kinds of authority, and knowing which one you are dealing with is the entire point.

DHARMA
It is written in a named text

A specific scripture, chapter or section that you could open and check for yourself.

Dharma means we can point to the text. Not "the scriptures say" — which scripture, and where in it. If we cannot name a source a reader could go and verify, the claim does not get this tag, no matter how widely it is believed or how confident we feel.

Dharma claims carry a confidence score. Consensus alone is never enough — practised everywhere is not the same as written somewhere.

FROM THE JANMASHTAMI GUIDE

"The midnight puja is performed during Nishita Kaal — the birth moment named in the text."

Bhagavata Purana · Skandha 10, Chapters 1–4 · corroborated in Vishnu Purana and Harivamsha Parva

PRATHA
It is how your region or family does it

Real, valid, worth keeping — and not from a text we can name.

Pratha is custom. It might be centuries old. It might be the most meaningful part of the day for your family. We are not ranking it below Dharma or suggesting you drop it.

We are only saying: this one is yours, not scripture's. Which matters when someone tells you your way is wrong, or that a different region's practice is the correct one. Neither is true. They are different customs, and both are legitimate.

FROM THE BILVA CONCEPT ARTICLE

"The smooth underside of the leaf faces the Shivalinga."

Widely observed across Shaiva practice · no named text mandates this orientation

BHRANTI
It is a misconception, and usually a frightening one

Corrected in plain language, in every guide where it appears.

Bhranti carries no score, because there is nothing to score. It is simply wrong, and it is usually wrong in a way that makes someone anxious — a warning about what happens if you get a step wrong, a rule that excludes people from their own practice, a fear attached to a date.

We print these in the guide for the ritual they attach to, not in a separate myths page nobody reads. If you have heard something frightening about a festival, the correction sits in the article about that festival.

FROM THE GANESH STHAPANA GUIDE

"Only a pandit can perform this correctly."

Nothing in the source tradition restricts household puja to priests. A pandit adds timing precision and convenience — not validity.

SECTION 02
What a confidence score means

Dharma and Pratha claims carry a score out of five. It describes how strong the textual basis is — not how important the ritual is, and not how strongly anyone believes it.

5/ 5★★★★★
Named consistently across several major texts

The same claim appears in more than one primary source, in compatible terms. Very little room for dispute.

4/ 5★★★★☆
Clearly stated in one major text

A named Purana or equivalent states it directly. Other sources may not address it either way.

3/ 5★★★☆☆
Present in the tradition's texts, but not specific to this ritual

A general form — a puja structure, a fasting principle — applied here by convention rather than named here.

2/ 5★★☆☆☆
Widely practised, documented, no named text

This is where most Pratha sits. Real and well established — just not scriptural.

1/ 5★☆☆☆☆
Oral or community tradition, minimal documentation

Passed down rather than written down. We say so plainly rather than dressing it up.

ONE THING WE DO NOT PUBLISH — YET
We have not published the internal rubric that assigns these scores.

There is one. It is written down, it is applied consistently, and every article passes through it before it goes live. What you see above is what each band means — not the decision rules behind it.

We are keeping those internal for now, while the method is still young and the platform is finding its footing. We intend to publish the full rubric once we have been in the open long enough to defend it properly — with a year of articles behind it rather than a handful.

We would rather tell you that plainly than let you assume there is no rubric at all. If a specific score looks wrong to you, the route to challenge it is at the bottom of this page, and it is open today.

SECTION 03
Why Panchang carries no tag and no score

Panchang content — today's tithi, a festival date, sunrise, Rahu Kaal timing — carries no classification tag and no confidence score. Not because it is less reliable, but because it is a different kind of claim entirely.

A tithi is computed, not interpreted. It is astronomy expressed in the tradition's own vocabulary. There is no scriptural authority to weigh, because nobody is making a claim about what you should do — only about where the Sun and Moon are.

Where a Panchang article does make a claim about practice — that Sutak applies only where an eclipse is visible, for instance — that specific claim is tagged and corrected like any other. The data is not. The interpretation is.

Our Panchang values are computed for Delhi-NCR on the Purnimanta convention, sourced from Drik Panchang, and verified manually before publication. Where your city's sunrise gives a different date, we say so.

SECTION 04
Where our sources come from

We work from primary texts and established editions, not from aggregator websites. Where a claim rests on a secondary source, we say so on the article.

PRIMARY TEXTS

Shiva Purana · Bhagavata Purana · Skanda Purana · Vishnu Purana · Bhavishya Purana · Mahabharata including Harivamsha Parva · Krishna Yajurveda (Shri Rudram) · Atharvaveda · Shatapatha Brahmana

Cited by name, samhita and chapter wherever the text is divided that way.

DHARMASHASTRA & STOTRA

Dharmasindhu · Agama and Dharmashastra puja procedure · traditional stotra canon including the Bilvashtakam

Used for ritual structure — the sixteen-step puja format, muhurat convention, offering protocol.

EDITIONS WE WORK FROM

Gita Press, Gorakhpur · Chaukhamba Sanskrit Pratishthan

Where translations differ materially, we note it on the article rather than picking one silently.

CALENDAR DATA

Drik Panchang — computed for Delhi-NCR, Purnimanta convention

Entered and verified manually. We do not auto-fetch, because a page served from the reader's own IP returns the wrong city's values.

SECTION 05
How an article gets made

Nothing goes live from a first draft.

1
Source-first drafting

The writer starts from the named text, not from what is commonly said about the ritual. Every claim is logged against its source before any prose is written.

2
Classification pass

Every element is tagged Dharma, Pratha or Bhranti and scored. Anything that cannot be tied to a named text routes to Pratha, regardless of how universal the practice is.

3
Citation verification

Each citation is checked against the original edition, not a website that quotes it. Claims that survive this stage keep their score; claims that do not are downgraded or dropped.

4
Regional variance check

A practitioner from the relevant tradition reviews whether the regional detail is accurately scoped — and whether we have quietly presented one region's custom as universal.

5
Fear audit

Every line is read once more for a single question: does this create, imply or reinforce fear? Lines that do are rewritten, even when factually correct.

6
Published — and revisable

An article going live is not the end of the process. Corrections are made openly, and the article notes when a claim has been revised.

SECTION 06
Three tests every line has to pass

Applied to every sentence on this platform, including this one.

TEST 1 · FEAR

Does this line create, imply, or reinforce fear about ritual practice?

If yes → rewrite. Always.
TEST 2 · CLARITY

If someone doing this for the first time read this, would they know exactly what to do?

If no → rewrite.
TEST 3 · SOURCE

If challenged, can we point to a specific text, chapter, or section for this claim?

If no → do not make the claim.
SECTION 07
What we will never do

These are not preferences. They are the conditions under which this platform is worth having.

Use fear to sell anything

No remedies for misfortune, no warnings about what happens if you skip a step, no dosha framed as a problem we can solve for a fee.

Let commerce change what we print

Selling a kit for a ritual does not alter a single word of the guide for it. The guide says a kit is unnecessary, because it is.

Present custom as scripture

If we cannot name the text, we say Pratha. Even when the practice is universal. Even when it would read better as Dharma.

Restrict practice by who you are

Where a source text places no restriction on who may perform a ritual, neither do we — and we correct claims that do.

Put a guide behind a paywall

Every ritual guide, every samagri list, every correction is free to read and will stay that way.

Quietly fix a mistake

Corrections are made on the article, visibly. We would rather be seen changing our mind than appear to have never been wrong.

SECTION 08
Tell us we are wrong
A method nobody can question is not a method

If a citation is wrong, a score looks too high, a regional practice is misrepresented, or something has been tagged Pratha that you can point to in a text — we want to hear it. Especially the last one.

Every challenge gets a reply from a person. Where you are right, the article changes and says that it changed.

1Tell us the article and the specific line.
2Tell us what you believe is correct, and where it comes from — a text, a regional tradition, a family practice.
3We check it against the source edition.
4You get a reply either way — including when we disagree, with our reasoning.

Nobody owns Dharma. Not a company, not an institution, not a person. We are students of this before we are publishers of it, and we read it that way — carefully, against the text, and without assuming the version we grew up with is the only one.

What we can promise is the method: name the text or do not make the claim, separate what is written from what is done, never use fear, and correct in the open. That is the whole of it. Everything else on this platform follows from those four things.
