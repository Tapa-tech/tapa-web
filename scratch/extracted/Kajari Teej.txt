Intro Prose
  ↳ Evergreen. Open with the positioning question — where does Kajari Teej sit in the Teej cycle — rather than a date. The three Teejs are what users are confused about; acknowledge the confusion before the article resolves it.

Three festivals carry the name Teej in the Hindu calendar. All three are for women. All three involve fasting and the worship of Shiva-Parvati. All three fall between July and September. And all three are persistently confused with each other online.
Kajari Teej is the middle one — fifteen days after Hariyali Teej, fourteen days before Hartalika Teej. It falls on the third day (Tritiya) of the dark fortnight (Krishna Paksha) of Bhadrapada. In North India it is called Badi Teej — the big Teej — because the observance is intense: a nirjala fast until moonrise, the worship of Neemdi Mata, and the preparation of sattu, all against a backdrop of the Kajri folk songs that give the festival its name.
What makes Kajari Teej distinct from the other two Teejs is not primarily scriptural — it is cultural. Where Hariyali Teej is rooted in the Shiva Purana and Hartalika Teej in the Shiva-Parvati reunion narrative, Kajari Teej's identity is carried primarily by folk tradition: the neem tree, the kund, the sattu, the songs. The puja structure is the same saubhagya vrat form as the other two. The spirit is identical. What distinguishes it is the texture of its practice.

Section: Why This Festival Exists — and How It Got Its Name
  ↳ Kajari Teej has no single founding Puranic narrative for this specific tithi. Present this honestly. The puja structure comes from the saubhagya vrat tradition. The name and identity come from the Kajri folk songs. The Neemdi Mata association is folk tradition. Explain all three sources without conflating them.

The name Kajari comes from Kajri — a genre of folk song sung in the Bhojpuri belt of Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Jharkhand during the monsoon season. Kajri songs are about separation and longing: a woman missing her husband, or a bride in her maternal home watching the rains without him. Sung under swings, in courtyards, in groups, they are among the oldest surviving folk traditions of this region. The festival of Kajari Teej takes its name from these songs — not the other way around.
The puja structure of Kajari Teej — a saubhagya vrat for marital harmony, fasting, the worship of Shiva and Parvati, the offering of sindoor and shringar items — is the same structure shared by all three Teej festivals. This structure comes from the Dharmashastra and Agama tradition of saubhagya vrats: fasts observed by married women for the long life and wellbeing of their husbands. What Kajari Teej adds to this structure is specific to itself: the neem tree, the clay kund, the sattu, the moonrise fast-break.
Neemdi Mata is a form of the Divine Mother associated specifically with Kajari Teej. The neem branch installed in the clay kund represents her presence. Neem is widely associated across India with protection, health, and the cooling of fever — qualities attributed to the Divine Mother. The worship of Neemdi Mata at Kajari Teej is oral tradition rather than textual prescription; it belongs to the same layer of living practice as the Kajri songs and the sattu offering.

The most common confusion is between Kajari and Hariyali Teej. They are fifteen days apart, both involve Shiva-Parvati worship, both involve fasting, and both are celebrated primarily in North India. The practical distinctions are three: Kajari falls in the dark fortnight (Krishna Paksha), its fast is broken at moonrise not the next morning, and its signature practices — neem, sattu, kajri songs — have no equivalent in Hariyali Teej.
The confusion between Kajari and Hartalika Teej is less common but also significant. Both fall in Bhadrapada, but Hartalika is in the Shukla Paksha two weeks later, has a 24-hour nirjala fast, and takes its name from a specific Puranic episode (the abduction of Parvati by her friend). Kajari is in the Krishna Paksha, breaks at moonrise, and is named for a folk song form, not a Puranic event.

Section: The Vidhi — Step by Step
  ↳ 7 steps. The Neemdi Mata puja and the moonrise arghya sequence are specific to Kajari Teej and must be described in detail. The fast-break condition (moonrise, not next morning) must be stated explicitly.

1
Bathe before sunrise and dress in green
Rise early and bathe before the fast begins. Wear green traditional attire — green is common to all three Teej festivals. Apply mehendi and prepare for the full day.
 [Dharma — preparatory]  [Green attire — Pratha] 
2
Prepare the sattu
Mix roasted gram flour with jaggery and pure ghee. This will be offered to Neemdi Mata in the evening and shared as prasad after the fast. Some households also prepare chapatis with jaggery and ghee for the cow feeding.
 [Pratha — Kajari-specific] 
3
Take the Vrat Sankalp
Sit before the Shiva-Parvati altar. Make a sincere resolve to keep the fast through the day until moonrise.
 [Dharma] 
4
Morning Shiva-Parvati puja
Set up idols or images of Shiva, Parvati, and Ganesha. Perform abhishek with water and milk. Offer sindoor, kumkum, and shringar items to Parvati. Offer flowers, incense, and light the diya.
 [Dharma — core puja] 
5
Prepare and worship Neemdi Mata in the evening
As evening approaches, prepare the puja space. Make a small clay kund (or use a vessel) and plant a fresh neem branch upright in it. Pour raw milk and water into the kund. Place the sattu, lemon, cucumber, and whole rice on the puja thali. Apply sindoor, kumkum, and mehndi to the neem branch. Light a diya near the kund. Worship Neemdi Mata — offer the sindoor and shringar, recite the Kajari Teej vrat katha.
 [Pratha — Kajari-specific] 
6
Offer arghya to the Moon at moonrise
When the moon rises, offer arghya — a mixture of water and milk — facing the moon. In many traditions, hold a silver ring and wheat grains while offering. Perform three or four pradakshina (clockwise rotations). Look at the moon's reflection in the milk-water of the kund — this is the traditional signal that the fast may be broken.
 [Dharma — moon worship]  [Arghya form — Pratha] 
7
Feed a cow, then break the fast
Feed the cow with the jaggery-ghee chapati (where this tradition is observed). Break the fast with water, then sattu prasad and other prepared foods — without garlic, onion, or non-vegetarian items.
 [Pratha — cow feeding]  [Dharma — fast break] 

Section: Myths & Facts

✕  "Kajari Teej is the same as Hariyali Teej, just observed two weeks later."
✓  They are distinct festivals. Hariyali Teej is in Shravana Shukla Paksha (waxing moon), rooted in the Shiva Purana, its signature practices are the green attire and jhula. Kajari Teej is in Bhadrapada Krishna Paksha (waning moon), rooted primarily in folk tradition, its signature practices are the neem tree, sattu, and kajri songs. Both worship Shiva-Parvati; neither is a variant of the other.

✕  "Kajari Teej is described in the Shiva Purana."
✓  No named chapter or verse in the Shiva Purana or any other Purana establishes Bhadrapada Krishna Tritiya specifically as a Shiva-Parvati event distinct from Hariyali Teej. Kajari Teej's identity is carried primarily by oral folk tradition from the Bhojpuri belt — the kajri songs, the Neemdi Mata worship, the sattu. The puja structure shares the saubhagya vrat tradition with the other Teej festivals, but that tradition is not unique to Kajari Teej.

Closing Prose
  ↳ 1-2 paragraphs. The arc: Kajari Teej is the monsoon Teej. Where Hariyali is the arrival of green, Kajari is the depths of the season — dark paksha, neem, sattu, kajri songs. It is the folk tradition's answer to the same devotion.

Kajri songs are songs about separation. A woman in her mother's home, watching the monsoon arrive, missing her husband. They are sung under swings hung from trees heavy with rain-wet leaves. The form of the song — longing, the beauty of nature, the waiting — is the emotional texture of Kajari Teej itself.
The festival lands in the dark fortnight, the waning moon, the deeper monsoon. The neem tree is not a decorative choice — it is medicinal, protective, bitter in the way that life is sometimes bitter, and sacred to the Divine Mother in a tradition that has no Puranic text behind it but needs none. Neemdi Mata's presence is felt in the neem branch, the kund, the raw milk poured over roots. This is the oldest kind of practice: direct, local, unmediated by a text, and alive because it has been lived continuously.
