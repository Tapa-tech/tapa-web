Primary Source
Mahabharata (Adi Parva) — the Sarpa Satra narrative of King Janamejaya and sage Astika; Bhagavata Purana — the Kaliya subduing narrative. Skanda Purana and Agni Purana additionally mention Naga worship in Shravan. Bhavishyottara Purana — Ashta Naga mantra.
Regional Variance
Widely observed across India but with genuine regional divergence: North India — clay or metal Naga idols, milk offering; Maharashtra — home and temple worship, anthill offerings; Bengal — Manasa Puja rooted in regional Manasa Mangal Kavya, not pan-Hindu shastra; Karnataka / Kerala — Sarpakkavu (serpent groves) and floor-drawn Naga images. Offering medium (milk, honey, turmeric, flowers), live snake vs. idol vs. drawn image, anthill visiting, fasting form — all Pratha or folk custom. The act of worship directed at Naga deities is the core.

Intro Prose
  ↳ Evergreen — do not add any year, date, or day of week here. Those live in the Panchang Card only.

Nag Panchami falls on the fifth day (Panchami) of Shukla Paksha in the month of Shravana — two days after Hariyali Teej. What most people encounter about this festival online is a mix: fear about what happens if you don't observe it, claims about snakes drinking milk, commercial packages promising to fix astrological doshas. Almost none of this is what the tradition is actually asking for.

Section: What the Day Commemorates
  ↳ Two scriptural narratives, each with source tag. Do not combine or reorder.

Narrative 1 of 2
The Sarpa Satra — Mahabharata, Adi Parva
King Janamejaya, son of Parikshit, organized the Sarpa Satra — a massive yajna aimed at destroying the entire Naga race in revenge for his father's death at the fangs of Takshaka. The sacrifice was working; serpents by the thousands were drawn into the fire. It was the young Brahmin sage Astika who intervened, using his learning and calm persuasion to convince Janamejaya to stop the yajna. He did. The day the Sarpa Satra was halted was Shravana Shukla Panchami. The festival that emerged from this moment is not about fear of snakes — it commemorates the act that saved them. Restraint over revenge. Coexistence over destruction.
 [Dharma · 4/5]  [Mahabharata Adi Parva] 

Narrative 2 of 2
The Second Narrative: Krishna and Kaliya — Bhagavata Purana
The Bhagavata Purana carries a second associated story: young Krishna's confrontation with Kaliya, the multi-hooded serpent who had poisoned the Yamuna River, making it dangerous for the people of Gokul. Krishna did not kill Kaliya. He danced upon his heads until the serpent was subdued, then showed him mercy — asking him to leave the river and return to his home in the ocean. The villagers were safe; the serpent was alive. The narrative is not one of punishment or fear. It is of power exercised with mercy, and of a river restored.
 [Dharma · 4/5]  [Bhagavata Purana]

Callout Card — A note on live snake worship
  ↳ Amber background callout — not a myth card. Welfare note. Do not move this below the myth cards.

⚠️  A note on live snake worship
In some rural areas, snake charmers bring cobras to temples on Nag Panchami, and devotees offer milk directly to the live animal. The tradition is declining for important reasons: snakes do not biologically drink milk, and forcing it causes harm. Animal welfare organisations have for years documented injury to the snakes involved in these practices.  The scriptural tradition asks for reverence toward Nagas as divine beings — not for interaction with wild snakes. Worshipping a clay idol, a stone image, or a Shivalinga (where the serpent Vasuki is always present around Shiva) fulfils the intent of the day completely and with harm to no living creature.

Section: Myths & Facts
  ↳ 4 myth cards. Pink row = the false claim. White row = the correction. Do not edit myth text without re-checking against the tagging log.

✕  "If you don't worship on Nag Panchami, a snake will bite someone in your family."
✓  No Puranic or Dharmashastra text makes this claim. The festival commemorates compassion and coexistence with serpents — not appeasement under threat. Fear-based messaging inverts the entire spirit of the day.

✕  "Snakes drink the milk you offer them, and this pleases the serpent god."
✓  Snakes are lactose-intolerant reptiles — they do not drink milk. Milk offered to a live snake is not absorbed and can cause illness. Milk in this tradition is offered to a Naga idol as a symbol of purity — not to a physical animal. The offering is devotional, not transactional.

✕  "Worshipping on this day cures Kaal Sarp Dosha in your horoscope."
✓  No Puranic source establishes this. Nag Panchami predates the modern astrological framing of Kaal Sarp Dosha by centuries. The association is a commercial-era addition, not a traditional or textual one. The festival can be observed for its own meaning without needing a Dosha to fix.

✕  "You must worship a live cobra — an idol doesn't have the same power."
✓  The tradition's core is reverence directed toward Naga deities as divine beings. A clay idol, a stone image, a silver Naga, or the serpent imagery on any Shivalinga all fulfil this intent. No text restricts the merit of worship to contact with live animals.

Closing Prose
  ↳ 1–2 paragraphs. Philosophical close. No CTA, no links. Do not add year-specific content.

Nag Panchami is perhaps the Hindu calendar's most direct expression of a principle the tradition holds consistently: that the natural world deserves reverence, not exploitation. Serpents eat rodents that destroy crops. They are part of the ecological web. Cultures that understood this built a day in the calendar to honour that relationship, and wrapped it in mythology that asked for compassion even toward creatures that frightened them.
That is the tradition. Not fear. Not dosha. Not a transactional exchange with an animal. A day of acknowledging that the world contains powers and creatures beyond human control, and that the correct response to that is reverence — not anxiety.
